The Art of Hand-Finishing in Haute Horlogerie: Anglage, Perlage & Geneva Stripes

A 10x magnifying loupe exploration into inward corner hand-beveling, zinc-plate black polishing, circular graining, and flame-blued screws.

In the commercial watch industry, a movement’s value is often advertised through numbers: beat rates, jewels count, and power reserve hours. Yet, among seasoned horological connoisseurs, the true soul and prestige of a movement is judged not through marketing specifications, but through the lens of a 10x magnifying loupe. What separates an industrial machine-assembled caliber from a true haute horlogerie masterpiece is the presence of hundreds of hours of patient, human hand-finishing (finissage).

Hand-finishing serves both aesthetic and functional purposes: it eliminates microscopic burrs that could break off into gear teeth, seals raw metal surfaces against corrosion, creates light-refracting textures, and elevates micro-mechanical mechanisms into museum-grade kinetic sculptures. In this definitive guide, our master decoration artisans dissect the most revered hand-finishing techniques practiced in our Swiss workshop.

1. Anglage (Hand-Beveling): The Ultimate Test of Master Craft

When a bridge or plate is milled from raw brass or German silver, its edges are sharp and perpendicular (90 degrees). Anglage is the delicate craft of filing away this sharp edge to create a uniform, 45-degree chamfer that is then polished to a mirror shine:

  • The Artisan’s Tools: The finisher uses handheld steel files of progressively finer grit, followed by wooden pegs made of elderberry pith, boxwood, and gentian roots coated with diamond polishing paste.
  • The Inward Angle (Angle Rentrant): Automated five-axis CNC milling machines can easily create rounded external corners. However, a sharp inward corner (where two bevel lines meet in an internal point of zero radius) cannot be machined by rotating milling cutters; it can only be executed by a master artisan using a sharp graver tool. The number of inward angles in a caliber is the single most reliable indicator of authentic hand-finishing.

“Machines can produce precision, but only human hands guided by an artisan’s eye can impart the warm, luminous soul of hand-anglage to cold metal.”

2. Poli Noir (Black Polishing / Specular Finish)

Also known as mirror polishing (poli miroir) or block polishing, black polishing is the most demanding surface treatment in horology, reserved exclusively for tourbillon bridges, steel balance cocks, and screw heads:

The artisan secures the steel component into a wooden peg and rubs it in figure-eight motions against a perfectly flat zinc lap plate coated with microscopic diamond paste (sub-micron particle size). The goal is to achieve absolute optical flatness with zero microscopic scratches or rounded edges. When viewed under direct ambient light, the surface reflects light in a single direction: from one angle, it reflects blinding white light; from all other angles, it absorbs light, appearing as a deep, mysterious obsidian void.

3. Côtes de Genève (Geneva Stripes)

Côtes de Genève consists of parallel wave-like ribbons decorated across movement bridges. In a high-end atelier, this is executed using a spinning boxwood lap or polyurethane peg mounted on a manual pantograph machine.

The artisan guides the rotating tool across the bridge surface in overlapping strokes with consistent manual pressure. The resulting texture creates microscopic diffraction grooves that catch ambient light, producing shimmering bands of brilliance that dance across the caliber as the watch moves on the wrist. In authentic haute horlogerie, the stripes align seamlessly across multiple independent bridges as if drawn across a single continuous canvas.

4. Perlage (Circular Graining)

Perlage (or stippling) consists of tightly overlapping circular patterns applied to movement baseplates and internal recesses. The craftsman presses a revolving rubber or wooden peg charged with abrasive diamond paste down onto the plate, holding it in place for a fraction of a second before lifting and moving horizontally by half a peg diameter.

This requires extraordinary rhythmic coordination and visual discipline to ensure that every individual circle possesses identical depth, diameter, and overlap. Beyond its sparkling aesthetic, perlage historically served a functional purpose: the microscopic circular grooves trapped airborne dust particles during assembly, preventing them from migrating into delicate balance wheel pivots.

5. Thermally Blued Screws & Sunk Screw Jewels (Moulures)

Even the smallest fasteners in an Orbit Dial Crest caliber receive meticulous hand decoration:

  1. Chamfered & Polished Screw Slots: Every screw head is hand-beveled along its outer circumference and inside the drive slot, eliminating sharp edges that could burr when turned with a screwdriver.
  2. Thermal Flame-Bluing: Polished steel screws are heated over an alcohol flame on a brass plate containing brass shavings. At exactly 290°C to 310°C, the steel forms an optical iron oxide layer that transforms the metal into a rich, permanent cornflower cobalt blue.
  3. Mirror-Polished Jewel Sinks (Moulures): The recessed seating holes for synthetic ruby jewels are mirror-polished using diamond-coated sphere tools, creating gleaming golden or silver halos around each red jewel bearing.

Frequently Asked Questions on Watch Decoration

How can I tell the difference between machine-beveled and hand-beveled edges? +
Under a 10x loupe, machine-beveled edges display tiny microscopic tool ridges and rounded interior corners. Authentic hand-anglage displays a glassy, mirror-polished surface with razor-sharp internal and external angles.
Does hand-finishing improve the timing accuracy of a watch? +
Yes, indirectly. Polishing pinion leaves and gear teeth reduces mechanical friction and optimizes energy transmission from the mainspring to the escapement, resulting in higher balance amplitude and superior chronometric stability.
